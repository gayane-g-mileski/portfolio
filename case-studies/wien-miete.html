01 — Brief

A Vienna Hausverwaltung does not have one rent question, it has four hundred of them. Every unit in a building sits under its own regime — Richtwert, Kategorie, angemessen or free market — decided by when the building was permitted, when that particular contract was signed, and whether public housing subsidies from anywhere between 1948 and today are still running against it.

The work is not hard so much as repetitive and unforgiving. The same handful of moves for every unit: building age, subsidy, contract date, fitments. Get one wrong and the consequence surfaces years later, when an over-charged rent can still be rolled back.

Mietzins-Check takes that classification off the desk. It names the regime, cites the provision it relied on, shows the derivation, and produces a report that can go into the file — for a single unit in about a minute, or for a whole portfolio from a CSV.

Hausverwaltungen

Running hundreds of units at once, each under a different regime. They need a defensible ceiling per unit and a paper trail for the file, the owners' meeting and, if it comes to it, the Schlichtungsstelle.

Immobilientreuhänder

Assessing a building before it changes hands. What the stock may lawfully return decides the yield — and it has to be known before the offer, not after.

02 — Which law applies

Everything downstream hangs on one branch: how far the Mietrechtsgesetz reaches. Two dates settle it. The building permit puts the property in one of three eras — Altbau up to 8 May 1945, the post-war window to 30 June 1953, Neubau after it. The contract date then decides the ceiling: from 1 March 1994 the Richtwert applies, between 1 January 1982 and 28 February 1994 the Kategoriemietzins, and anything older keeps the rent agreed in the old contract.

Neither date is on a rent statement. So the app looks them up — building year from the City of Vienna's building data, the address from the city's own address service — and where the year cannot be resolved it says so plainly and offers a ready-made enquiry to the MA 25 rather than guessing.

03 — The rent-check workflow

The flow is ordered by what the calculation needs next, not by what is easy to ask. Address first, because it resolves the district and pulls the building year; then the type of unit, the floor area, the fitments, and finally the contract date that fixes the ceiling. Each answer narrows the result immediately, so the classification never lands as a surprise.

Two decisions did most of the work. The address is optional — without it the location surcharge simply drops out of the estimate and the app says so, rather than blocking on a field the user may not have to hand. And where an answer is missing or contradictory, the flow asks again for exactly that gap instead of failing silently or filling it in.

Surcharges are the part that invites a bad design. It is tempting to itemise every fitting and add the amounts up, but § 16 Abs 2 MRG requires an overall assessment and the Oberste Gerichtshof has rejected exactly that itemise-and-sum approach (RIS-Justiz RS0117881). So the result is a corridor around the base value, not a false-precision single number.

04 — Before and after

The comparison that mattered was not against another calculator. It was against how the work is done today: the lease, a look at comparables, a call to a lawyer, and an estimate nobody wants to put in writing — repeated once per unit.

At portfolio scale that stops being viable, so the whole thing runs from a CSV: address, area, category, building year, contract date and current rent are enough. Roughly four hundred units come back in about twenty minutes, as a table and as a CSV, flagging which ones look to be sitting above the line.

The constraint that shaped this part was confidentiality, not speed. A portfolio list is third-party personal data, so the calculation runs entirely in the browser and the file never leaves the machine — which is also what makes the Art. 28 GDPR processing agreement a short document rather than a difficult one.

05 — Information architecture

The product carries two jobs that pull in different directions: a stranger checking one unit with no account, and a firm working a portfolio every week. The free single check stays the front door — no sign-up, nothing to configure — while everything built for volume sits behind Für Profis: CSV portfolios, indexation, team access and shared object lists, a JSON API with a versioned calculation logic, and a white-label iframe for firms who want the calculator on their own site under their own brand.

Pricing follows the same split rather than cutting across it. The calculator is free and stays free; the €24 PRO report and the €49-a-month Profi tier charge for the two things that actually carry into the working day — the document for the file, and working with whole portfolios.
