01 — Brief

A Vienna property manager does not have one rent question. They have four hundred. Every unit sits under its own rules — set by when the building was permitted, when that tenancy was signed, and whether public money from the last seventy years is still running against it.

And not only flats: shops, a converted loft, an extension, a company flat, a care home, a holiday let, a garage. Fourteen kinds of tenancy, each with its own answer.

Mietzins-Check names the rules, quotes the provision, shows its working. One unit in about a minute; four hundred in about twenty.

Hausverwaltungen

Hundreds of units, each under different rules — and a ceiling per unit they can stand behind, in writing, for the file and the owners' meeting.

Immobilientreuhänder

Sizing up a building before it changes hands. What the units may lawfully earn decides the yield, and you need that before the offer.

02 — Which law applies

Everything hangs on how far the rent act reaches, and two dates settle it: the building permit, then the contract.

Neither is printed on a rent statement, so the app fetches them from the city's own data. When a date or a subsidy cannot be resolved it says so and hands over a ready-written enquiry to the MA 25, the MA 50 or the federal housing fund — public money from 1948 on still changes the answer, and no other calculator even asks.

03 — The rent-check workflow

The questions come in the order the calculation needs them, not the order that is easiest to ask — address first because it resolves the district and the building year, contract date last because it sets the ceiling.

The address is optional: leave it out and the location supplement drops away, and the app says so rather than blocking on a field you may not have. Where an answer is missing it asks again for that one thing instead of guessing.

Supplements are where it would be easy to build the wrong thing — itemise every fitting, add up the amounts. § 16 Abs 2 MRG asks for an overall judgement instead, and the Supreme Court has rejected the adding-up (RS0117881).

04 — The result

A number alone is useless here. Nobody takes “€7.04” to an owners' meeting.

So the regime leads, the provision is named, and the figure arrives as a band — with the derivation, three viewpoints on it, and links into the federal legal information system so the reader can check the wording.

The same unit is then read four ways, because the question behind the question is rarely just the rent.

Price band

The ceiling as a corridor, with the derivation and three viewpoints on it: case law, what the arbitration board tends to accept, and the market.

Indexation

When this unit may next be raised and by how much — under the current cap of 1% for 2026 and at most 2% for 2027.

Yield

The whole sum for a buy-to-let: purchase costs, vacancy, maintenance reserve, depreciation, and repairs written off over fifteen years.

Operating costs

The § 21 MRG catalogue, line by line — what may be passed on to tenants and what is maintenance and may not.

Every result is stamped with the date the Richtwert was published and the version of the logic that produced it. A report sitting in a file two years from now should say which rules it was working from.

05 — One unit, then four hundred

The thing to beat was never another calculator. It was the lease, a few comparables, a call to a lawyer, and a figure nobody wants to put in writing — once per unit.

At portfolio size that stops working, so the whole thing runs from a spreadsheet. Around four hundred units come back in roughly twenty minutes, with the ones above the line flagged.

Confidentiality shaped this part, not speed. A portfolio list is other people's personal data, so the calculation runs in the browser and the file never leaves the machine — which is also why the Article 28 agreement is a short document. Indexation, increase letters in series, team access, a versioned API and a white-label build sit on the same tier.

06 — What it is not

In a regulated field, overclaiming is the fastest way to lose a professional audience. This is not legal advice, not a valuation, not the work of a licensed property trustee. A binding ruling comes from the city's arbitration board, and the product says so on the page rather than in the small print.

So the comparison with the city's own calculator is printed openly, including the rows where the answer is no.

07 — Where the numbers come from

Two kinds of number go in, and they are not equally solid. The published values are official; the location supplement and market rents are approximations by district. The app says which is which and links to the map that binds.

Published values follow each official notice; the approximations are reviewed at least once a year.

08 — Two doors, two prices

The product does two jobs that pull in opposite directions: a stranger checking one unit with no account, and a firm working through a portfolio every week. The free single check stays the front door — no sign-up, nothing to configure — and everything built for volume sits behind the professional tier.

Pricing follows the same split. The calculator is free and stays free. The €24 report and the €49-a-month professional tier charge for the two things that carry into the working day: the document for the file, and working through whole portfolios. Prices are gross, VAT is shown net beside them, EU businesses with a valid VAT number are invoiced without it, and the fourteen-day right of withdrawal is spelled out — including that it lapses the moment you ask for the report immediately.
