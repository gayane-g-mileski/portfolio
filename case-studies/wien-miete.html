Wien Miete — Is My Rent Lawful?

Austrian rent law routes every Vienna flat into a different pricing regime, and each lawful rent is assembled from surcharges and deductions that almost nobody can reconstruct from their contract. Wien Miete walks a tenant through that calculation in plain language — and returns a clear verdict.

01 — Brief

Vienna is a city of tenants. Roughly three quarters of households rent, a large share of the stock is regulated, and the rules that decide what a landlord may charge are among the most intricate in Europe. The result is a very ordinary question that is remarkably hard to answer: is the rent I pay every month actually lawful?

The difficulty is not that the law is secret. It is published, and advice bodies explain it well. The difficulty is that the answer is assembled: first the flat has to be routed into the right legal regime, then a base value has to be found for the right point in time, then a series of qualitative surcharges and deductions have to be argued one by one. Miss any step and the number you end up with is meaningless.

Wien Miete takes that assembly line and turns it into a guided conversation. The tenant answers plain questions about their flat and their contract; the app keeps track of which regime applies, what the law adds and subtracts, and where the answer is still uncertain — and ends with a verdict they can act on.

The regime that applies is decided by the building permit date and the contract date — neither of which is printed on a rent bill.
The surcharges and deductions are qualitative judgements, not a table you can look up.
The Richtwert is revalorised periodically, so the lawful figure depends on when the contract was signed.
Tenants compare the gross amount they transfer with a net statutory cap, and conclude the wrong thing.
Fixed-term contracts carry a statutory deduction that is very often simply not applied.
Challenging a rent runs through the Schlichtungsstelle or the courts, so people want confidence before they start.
02 — Which law applies

Everything downstream depends on one branch point: how far the Mietrechtsgesetz applies to this particular flat. That is decided by facts a tenant rarely has to hand — when the building was permitted, when the contract was signed, how large the flat is — rather than by anything printed on the monthly bill.

Rather than asking the tenant to self-classify, the app infers the regime from ordinary questions and then tells them which set of rules it is applying, and why.

Altbau · Vollanwendung MRG
Richtwertmietzins

The main system for flats in buildings whose building permit predates 8 May 1945, where the contract was concluded from 1 March 1994 onwards. A statutory base value per square metre is adjusted up and down for the specific flat.

Contracts before March 1994
Kategoriemietzins

Older tenancies are still capped by the category system, which grades a flat A to D by its fitments and condition rather than by a location-adjusted base value.

Large flats · listed buildings
Angemessener Mietzins

A "reasonable" market-referenced rent applies to particular cases — for example flats over 130 m² of usable floor area, or listed buildings after substantial investment.

Teil- / Vollausnahme
Free market

Where the MRG applies only partly or not at all — typically new builds permitted after 30 June 1953, small owner-occupied buildings and holiday lets — the rent is negotiated freely.

03 — How a lawful rent is assembled

Inside the Richtwert system a lawful rent is not looked up — it is built. A statutory base value per square metre is adjusted upwards for advantages and downwards for defects, and only then can it be compared with what is actually being charged.

  1. 1
    Start from the statutory base value
    The Richtwert is set per federal state and revalorised periodically, so any calculation has to be pinned to the value in force on the day the contract was signed.
  2. 2
    Add location surcharge (Lagezuschlag)
    Districts better than the average attract a surcharge, which the landlord must have disclosed in writing at the time of contract.
  3. 3
    Add fitment and layout surcharges
    Balcony, terrace or garden, floor level and lift, storage, and the standard of the fitments each move the figure up.
  4. 4
    Subtract condition deductions
    Poor maintenance, noise exposure, awkward layouts and a missing lift all pull the lawful figure back down.
  5. 5
    Subtract the fixed-term deduction
    A fixed-term (befristet) tenancy carries a statutory 25 % deduction on the net rent for as long as it stays fixed-term.
  6. 6
    Separate the other lines on the bill
    Only the net main rent is capped this way. Operating costs are billed separately and residential rent carries 10 % VAT — comparing gross figures is the most common mistake.
04 — The rent-check workflow

The core design problem was sequencing. A tenant cannot be asked for a location surcharge before the app knows the flat is in the Richtwert system, and cannot be asked about fitments before it knows what the fitments are worth. So the flow is ordered by what the calculation needs next, not by what is convenient to ask.

Each stage collects one kind of fact and shows its effect immediately, so the verdict at the end never arrives as a surprise. Where an answer is missing or contradictory the flow loops back and asks again for exactly that gap — it never fails silently or guesses.

05 — Before and after

The comparison that mattered most was not against another app — it was against what a tenant does today: piecing an answer together from the contract, a rent bill, a couple of advice pages and, often, a phone call. It usually ends in an estimate nobody trusts enough to act on.

06 — Information architecture

The check itself is one journey, but it has to be re-enterable — people stop when they cannot find their contract and come back later. Everything else in the app exists to support that: saved checks, the plain-language explanations, and the documents produced at the end.

07 — Result states

A rent check has three honest outcomes, and the third one matters as much as the other two. Where the evidence is thin the app says so rather than manufacturing confidence — an over-confident "your rent is illegal" would send someone into a formal dispute on bad footing.

The verdict screen always shows the calculation that produced it, so the tenant can see which surcharge or deduction is doing the work and take that to an advice body or the Schlichtungsstelle.

08 — Design language

The visual language is deliberately warm. Tenancy disputes are stressful and the subject matter is legal, so the palette is built from caffè-latte tones — espresso through cream — rather than the cold blues and alarm reds that legal-tech products default to.

One accent green carries all the way through, and it is rationed: it marks the verdict, the active path and a lawful result, and nothing else. That way the single colour a tenant is looking for is never competing with decoration.
