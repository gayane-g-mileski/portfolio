For Vienna's property managers and agents: is this flat Richtwert, Kategorie, angemessen or free? About a minute, with the law it relied on, how it got there, and a report you can put straight in the file.

01 — Brief

A Vienna property manager does not have one rent question. They have four hundred. Every flat in a building sits under its own rules, and which ones depend on when the building was permitted, when that particular tenancy was signed, and whether any public subsidy from the last seventy years is still running against it.

None of it is difficult. It is just the same handful of steps, over and over, with no room to slip: building age, subsidy, contract date, fittings. Get one wrong and you may not hear about it for years — and an overcharged rent can still be clawed back.

Mietzins-Check takes that off the desk. It tells you which rules apply, quotes the provision, shows its working, and gives you a report for the file — one flat in about a minute, or a whole portfolio from a spreadsheet.

Hausverwaltungen

Hundreds of flats at once, each under different rules. They need a ceiling per flat they can stand behind, and something in writing for the file, the owners' meeting and, if it goes that far, the arbitration board.

Immobilientreuhänder

Sizing up a building before it changes hands. What the flats may lawfully earn decides the yield — and you need to know that before you make the offer, not after.

02 — Which law applies

Everything hangs on one question: how far the rent act reaches. Two dates settle it. The building permit puts the property in one of three eras — pre-May 1945, the post-war window to mid-1953, or newer. The contract date then sets the ceiling: from March 1994 the Richtwert, between 1982 and early 1994 the Kategorie rent, and anything older keeps whatever the old contract said.

Neither date is printed on a rent statement, so the app goes and finds them — the building year from the City of Vienna's own data, the address from the city's address service. When the year cannot be found it says so, and hands you a ready-written enquiry to the city rather than guessing.

03 — The rent-check workflow

The questions come in the order the calculation needs them, not the order that is easiest to ask. Address first, because it gives you the district and the building year; then the type of flat, the size, the fittings, and last the contract date that fixes the ceiling. Each answer narrows the answer on screen, so nothing lands as a surprise at the end.

Two decisions did most of the work here. The address is optional — leave it out and the location supplement simply drops away, and the app tells you it has. And when something is missing or does not add up, it asks again for that one thing instead of quietly failing or filling it in for you.

Supplements are where it would be easy to build the wrong thing. The tempting version itemises every fitting and adds up the amounts — but the law asks for an overall judgement, and the Supreme Court has thrown out exactly that add-it-up approach. So the answer is a range around the base figure, not a made-up exact number.

04 — Before and after

The thing to beat was never another calculator. It was how this gets done today: read the lease, look at a few comparables, ring a lawyer, and end up with a figure nobody wants to put in writing. Once per flat.

At portfolio size that stops working, so the whole thing runs from a spreadsheet — address, size, category, building year, contract date and current rent are enough. Around four hundred flats come back in roughly twenty minutes, as a table and a file, with the ones sitting above the line flagged.

What shaped this part was confidentiality, not speed. A portfolio list is other people's personal data, so the sums happen in the browser and the file never leaves the machine. That is also why the data processing agreement is a short document instead of a difficult conversation.

05 — Information architecture

The product does two jobs that pull in opposite directions: a stranger checking a single flat with no account, and a firm working through a portfolio every week. The free single check stays the front door — no sign-up, nothing to set up — and everything built for volume sits behind the professional tier: portfolios from a file, indexation, team access and shared lists, an API with versioned calculation rules, and an embeddable version for firms who want it on their own site under their own name.

Pricing splits the same way. The calculator is free and stays free. The €24 report and the €49-a-month professional tier charge for the two things that actually carry into the working day: the document for the file, and working through whole portfolios.
